J.S. Holliday

June 10, 1924 – August 31, 2006

Historian of the California Gold Rush

Jaquelin Smith Holliday II — “Jim” to nearly everyone who knew him, J.S. Holliday on the spines of his books — spent more than half a century listening to the voices of the men and women who came to California in 1849. He gave the Gold Rush its most human telling in The World Rushed In, led two of the state’s great historical institutions, appeared on screen in Ken Burns’ The West, and, with his wife Belinda Vidor Holliday, built a house above a quiet lake in the Sierra foothills not far from the country the forty-niners had crossed. He was, in the words of his friend Congressman Pete Stark, “the center of gravity” in any room he entered.

An Indiana Boyhood, a Navy War

Jim Holliday was born in Indianapolis on June 10, 1924, to Martha Henley Holliday and William J. Holliday, whose family was in the steel business. He was educated at private schools, including the Hill School in Pottstown, Pennsylvania.

The Second World War intervened. Holliday trained at the Navy’s midshipman school at Northwestern University and served as an officer in the Pacific aboard an escort aircraft carrier, the USS Santee. When the war ended he returned to Yale, where his studies had been interrupted, and graduated with a degree in history in 1948.

The Diary That Shaped a Life

It was at Yale that Holliday met the subject that would occupy him for the rest of his life. In his final year, the rare-book dealer Edward Eberstadt pointed him toward a set of papers in the university’s collection: the diary and letters of William Swain, a young farmer from Youngstown, New York, who set out in 1849 with a Michigan company called the Wolverine Rangers and made the seven-month overland crossing to the California gold fields. Swain had kept a faithful diary of the journey — across the Great Plains, along the Humboldt River, through Nevada’s Black Rock Desert — and his family back home had saved every letter he sent, along with their own replies.

Holliday admitted he was not impressed at first. Only after he had read the diaries of other forty-niners did he understand what he was holding: a rare two-sided record of the Gold Rush, the voice of the man who went and the voices of the family who waited. Swain’s surviving daughter, Sara Sabrina Swain, eventually entrusted the family’s letters to him.

In 1949 — the centennial of the Gold Rush — Holliday followed Swain west. “I came here for gold,” he liked to say, “and found other ways of seeking success in California.” He retraced Swain’s route, read small-town newspapers from the period, and went on to graduate study in history at the University of California, Berkeley, earning his doctorate in 1958. He spent a year as a research fellow at the Huntington Library in San Marino, became assistant director of Berkeley’s Bancroft Library — one of the great archives of the American West — and taught as an associate professor of history at San Francisco State. A colleague there called him one of the most vital people he ever knew.

Founding Director of the Oakland Museum

In 1967 Holliday was named the founding director of what would become the Oakland Museum of California, a new institution formed by joining the city’s public museum, its art gallery, and the Snow Museum of Natural History under one roof, in a landmark terraced building designed by Kevin Roche John Dinkeloo and Associates with gardens by Dan Kiley. The museum’s purpose, then as now, was to tell the story of California — its art, its history, and its natural world — in a single place.

Holliday believed that a museum telling California’s story had to answer to all of California’s people. He pressed the board to form a community advisory committee that would give Oakland’s African American residents a real voice in the museum’s direction. It was a position well ahead of its time, and it cost him his job: the board dismissed him months before the museum opened its doors in 1969. The firing caused an uproar among the staff, and the museum’s education director, Julia Hare, resigned in protest.

The California Historical Society

Holliday soon found another home, as executive director of the California Historical Society, a post he held twice, from 1970 to 1977 and again from 1983 to 1985. He later became the society’s executive director emeritus.

His first term produced one of the most consequential exhibitions in the society’s history. While at Oakland, Holliday had considered a show built around the government photographs of the forced removal and incarceration of Japanese Americans during World War II; when he moved to the Historical Society, the project — curated by Richard and Maisie Conrat — came with him. Executive Order 9066 opened in San Francisco in January 1972 and brought together dozens of photographs, many by Dorothea Lange, along with images by Ansel Adams and Toyo Miyatake. It went on to the Corcoran Gallery in Washington, the Whitney Museum of American Art in New York, and museums across the country, and later traveled to Hawai‘i and Japan. It confronted Americans with a chapter of their history many preferred not to see; a later director of the society recalled that it “caused quite a stir.” For a generation of younger museum leaders, Holliday became a model of the historian as public conscience.

The World Rushed In

Through all of it, Holliday kept working on the book. He received his first publishing contract in 1950; the editor who signed him retired before it was done, reportedly expecting it would appear only after its author’s death. Holliday read “miles of microfilm,” old newspapers, diaries, and letters, and limited himself to the accounts of people who had traveled the same trails as Swain at the same time. He was so exacting a writer that a colleague at the California State Library recalled it could take him a week to get two paragraphs right.

The World Rushed In: The California Gold Rush Experience was published by Simon & Schuster in 1981, some thirty years after he began it. Its structure was his great invention: Swain’s diary and letters carry the reader from Youngstown to the diggings and back, while the voices of other forty-niners, set alongside in brackets, widen a single journey into the story of a whole migration — what Holliday called the greatest peacetime mass migration in history. The book became a best-seller, ran to thirteen printings, and won the Silver Medal of the Commonwealth Club of California and the Oscar Lewis Award for Achievement in Western History from the Book Club of California. The historian Kevin Starr called it “a classic” and a “masterly narrative.” It was later reissued by the University of Oklahoma Press.

Holliday saw the Gold Rush not as the last chapter of the pioneer story but as something stranger: most of the forty-niners never meant to stay. They came to get rich and go home, and in doing so they set California permanently in motion and changed the country’s sense of what was possible.

Rush for Riches

For the Gold Rush sesquicentennial, Holliday returned to the institution he had founded. Rush for Riches: Gold Fever and the Making of California, co-published by the Oakland Museum of California and the University of California Press in 1999, carried the story far beyond 1849 — from Spain’s first sight of the coast through the industrialization of mining, the ruinous era of hydraulic mining, and on to the fiftieth-anniversary celebrations of 1898, by which time gold had become more symbol than source of wealth. Illustrated with some 250 images, it was praised for its command of a vast subject and for prose that read as though its author had seen the events himself.

Ken Burns’ The West

In 1996 Holliday appeared on camera in The West, the eight-part PBS series directed by Stephen Ives and executive-produced by Ken Burns, alongside historians including Stephen Ambrose and Richard White. The Gold Rush is the subject of the series’ third episode, Speck of the Future, covering 1848 to 1856. Burns called himself “a huge fan” and said simply: “No one writes better about California’s irresistible past.”

Belinda

In March 1982, at a signing for The World Rushed In, Holliday met the painter Belinda Vidor — daughter of the film director King Vidor and the actress Eleanor Boardman, who would later join the juried membership of the Carmel Art Association. Their first date followed that July. They were married on June 11, 1983, and she called him her “husband, her champion, her best friend, her dream come true.” He was, first and foremost, a family man, and his children and stepchildren regarded him as a mentor, a force, and a wonderful father.

The couple made their home in Carmel-by-the-Sea, and Jim threw himself into the life of the town. He helped found the Carmel Residents Association and was named its Citizen of the Year in 2001; he served as a trustee of Tor House, the poet Robinson Jeffers’ stone home on Carmel Point; and he was a member of San Francisco’s Bohemian and Roxburghe Clubs and the Zamorano Club of Los Angeles. Friends described him as forceful and outspoken yet gentle, endlessly curious, a great listener, and unusually generous in his praise of other writers. The San Francisco Chronicle remembered “a big, handsome, rumpled man with a passion for the rugged life.”

The Holliday Lake House

That passion found its fullest expression in the Sierra foothills. Above the small town of North Fork in Madera County — a town that calls itself the exact center of California, in the Gold Rush country Jim had spent his life studying — he and Belinda built a house beside a private lake.

They built it to speak to early California. Massive open beams span the main living space in soaring arches, structural and unhurried, giving the interior the particular silence of old timber and honest construction. A wide wrap-around veranda follows the house on all sides, oriented toward the water: a place for morning coffee as the mist lifts off the lake and for evenings when the light goes gold on the pines and the herons settle in the shallows. The house brought together both of its builders’ ways of seeing — the historian’s reverence for what came before, and the painter’s and filmmaker’s daughter’s instinct for what a place, properly seen, can reveal.

They chose not to landscape the land in any conventional sense. Left to its own life, it has become over the decades a sanctuary for the wildlife of the foothills: great blue herons working the shallows, acorn woodpeckers in the pines, osprey hunting the lake, wood ducks and mergansers in season, and mule deer at the water’s edge at dusk. It remains in the family today and welcomes guests as the Holliday Lake House.

Legacy

J.S. Holliday died at his home in Carmel on August 31, 2006, at the age of 82. He was survived by Belinda; and by his children Timothy Holliday, Martha Brett Holliday, and W.J. Holliday. A few weeks later his friend Representative Pete Stark rose to read a tribute to him into the Congressional Record, saying that with his death California had “lost part of our soul.” Gary Kurutz of the California State Library called him “a towering figure in California history.”

His books remain the place where readers meet the forty-niners as people — homesick, hopeful, frightened, stubborn, and very far from home. And above a still lake in the foothills, the house he built with Belinda still stands, its veranda still turned toward the water.
